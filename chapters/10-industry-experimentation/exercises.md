# Chapter 10 exercises: Industry experimentation

- Note: each exercise is small, runnable in the chapter's notebook or a fresh Python REPL, and probes one specific point the chapter raised. Two-lens exercises ask both questions explicitly.
- Exercise 1: Predict the day-7 vs day-30 verdict
  - Setup: paper and pencil first. Treatment is clickbait. Click rate lift is +10 percent on day 1 with exponential decay, retention lift is 0 on day 1 dropping to -5pp by day 30 with the slow decay curve from the chapter (clickbait_curves.png).
  - Task: write down, before plotting, your guess for the day-7 click-rate lift, the day-7 retention lift, and the sign of each metric at day 30. Then build the deterministic curves: click_lift = 0.10 * exp(-days / 7) and retention_lift = -0.05 * (1 - exp(-days / 14)) for days = np.arange(0, 31). Read off the values at day 7 and day 30.
  - Expected pattern: day 7 click lift roughly +0.037 (still meaningfully positive). Day 7 retention lift roughly -0.020 (negative but small). Day 30 click lift near 0.014, retention lift near -0.040. Both curves are deterministic cartoons, not fits to data, and the chapter flags this. The point: at the typical 1-week experiment horizon, the positive metric is still loud and the negative metric has barely started.
  - Two-lens: this exercise has no statistics. It is purely a metric-choice and time-horizon question. Write one sentence on each lens's role: neither lens helps if you measure the wrong metric for the wrong horizon.
  - Stretch: replace the exponential curves with a plateau (clicks plateau at +0.04, retention plateaus at -0.04 by day 30). Does the day-7 vs day-30 disagreement persist? The chapter notes real novelty curves vary in shape.
- Exercise 2: Aggregates hide the cohort that matters
  - Setup: three cohorts. Most engaged 10 percent with treatment_lift=-0.02 on a baseline of 0.40. Moderately engaged 30 percent with treatment_lift=+0.03 on a baseline of 0.20. Long tail 60 percent with treatment_lift=+0.06 on a baseline of 0.05.
  - Task: build SegmentSpec entries and call expkit.sim.user_segments.segmented_binary at n_total=10000, seed=1010. Compute per-cohort two_proportion_z and per-cohort point estimates. Compute the population-weighted aggregate lift by summing fraction * point_estimate.
  - Expected pattern: aggregate lift is dominated by the long tail (60 percent of users) and lands near +0.039 on average. Most engaged cohort is around -0.02 (your most valuable users got worse). This reproduces the structure in aggregate_hides_structure.png. The aggregate is technically true. The cohort breakdown is what would change the decision.
  - Two-lens: run the aggregate two-proportion z on the pooled data and compare to a Bayesian P(diff > 0) on the pooled data. Both will show a clear positive lift. Both miss the cohort regression. The lens is not the issue. The aggregation is.
  - Stretch: add value weights (most engaged users are 10x as valuable per session as the long tail). Recompute a value-weighted aggregate. Does the sign flip?
- Exercise 3: Guardrails and the false-alarm flood
  - Setup: 20 guardrail metrics, all flat (no true difference). Per-metric noise is normal with sigma=0.05. N=2000 per arm per metric. Seed 1020.
  - Task: run 2000 simulated experiments. In each experiment, simulate 20 metrics under the null and run a two_sample_t test on each. Count the fraction of experiments in which at least one of the 20 raw p-values is below 0.05 (naive). Compare to the family-wise rejection rate after Bonferroni adjustment with expkit.inference.multitest.bonferroni at alpha=0.05.
  - Expected pattern: naive rate around 0.64 (sample-to-sample noise around the independence upper bound). Bonferroni rate around 0.05. This reproduces the guardrails.png numbers and the chapter's caveat that 0.64 is the independence upper bound.
  - Two-lens: also run expkit.inference.multitest.holm and benjamini_hochberg. Holm should match Bonferroni's family-wise rate but admit slightly more rejections in expectation. BH controls FDR, not FWER, and will fire more often. Pick the right tool for the question your stakeholders actually want answered.
  - Stretch: introduce correlation between the 20 metrics (draw a single shared latent confounder and add 0.5 of it to each metric's per-user noise). The naive false-alarm rate should drop below 0.64 because the metrics now correlate. The chapter says real guardrail metrics share variance and the pathology inflates less than the upper bound suggests.
- Exercise 4: Hierarchical pooling tames the guardrail problem
  - Setup: same 20 guardrails, all flat, but now pretend you have run one experiment and recorded one point estimate and standard error per metric. Use the per-experiment estimates from one of your seeds in Exercise 3.
  - Task: fit a hierarchical Bayesian model in PyMC. Model sketch: tau_lift ~ HalfNormal(0.05), mu_lift ~ Normal(0, 0.02), lift_i ~ Normal(mu_lift, tau_lift), observed metric_i ~ Normal(lift_i, sigma_i). Sample 2000 draws, 2 chains, tune 1000, random_seed=1040. Report the posterior mean and 95 percent credible interval for each lift_i, alongside the raw per-metric two_sample_t point estimate and CI.
  - Expected pattern: each per-metric posterior mean is shrunk toward zero (the population mean) compared to its raw per-metric point estimate. The ones that were noisy outliers in Exercise 3 shrink the most. This is the partial pooling the chapter recommends as the proper Bayesian remedy. False alarms shrink because the model has learned that most of the 20 metrics are flat.
  - Two-lens: the frequentist analogue here is empirical Bayes shrinkage (James-Stein or its modern descendants). Note that the chapter's claim "Bayesian alone is not the answer" specifically targets per-metric flat-prior credible intervals, not hierarchical models. Pooling is the move, and "Bayesian" is the engine.
  - Stretch: replace the HalfNormal(0.05) on tau with HalfNormal(0.5). Watch the model trust the data more and shrink less. The hyperprior on tau is doing the work.
- Exercise 5: Predict, then ship the wrong thing
  - Setup: paper and pencil first. You have a 1-week experiment showing +4 percent clicks, p<0.001 (huge sample). 7-day retention is unchanged in the experiment window. The PM wants to ship.
  - Task: write down, before computing anything, your decision and one sentence of justification. Then sketch the day-30 retention curve assuming the click lift is novelty (decays to zero by day 30) and that the retention drag has the slow form of Exercise 1 (-0.05 by day 30). Read off the projected day-30 lift on each metric. Decide again.
  - Expected pattern: with novelty decay, the day-30 click lift is around +1 percent and the retention drag is roughly -5pp. The honest answer the chapter gives: "do not ship on a 1-week click win when the long-horizon metric is still in motion. Either extend the horizon, or pre-commit to a retention-based ship rule before the experiment starts."
  - Two-lens: a frequentist sequential test on retention would still be silent at day 7 because the effect has barely started. A Bayesian posterior on day-30 retention would be wide and centred near zero at day 7 because the data does not yet constrain the long-horizon effect. Both lenses are honest, and both fail to flag the issue if the horizon is wrong.
  - Stretch: pre-commit a retention-based ship rule. Write it as one decision rule using expkit.inference.normal.two_proportion_z with a one-sided test on day-30 retention at alpha=0.05. Note that the rule itself is not the hard part. The discipline of running the experiment to day 30 is.
- Exercise 6: Find a guardrail family size where Bonferroni kills you
  - Setup: vary the number of guardrails K from 5 to 200. True effect on the primary metric: lift = +0.02, sigma=0.10. K-1 guardrails are flat with sigma=0.05. N=2000 per arm. Power on the primary metric depends on K because Bonferroni shrinks the per-test alpha to 0.05/K.
  - Task: for each K in {5, 20, 50, 100, 200}, compute the Bonferroni-adjusted alpha for the primary metric. Use expkit.power.continuous.two_sample_z_power to compute the power on the primary metric at that alpha. Tabulate.
  - Expected pattern: at K=5, per-test alpha is 0.01 and power on a 2pp lift is comfortable. At K=200, per-test alpha is 0.00025 and power collapses. The chapter says "Bonferroni is conservative. At 100 tests, alpha/100 = 0.0005 per test, which kills power." Verify it.
  - Skeptical: replace Bonferroni with Benjamini-Hochberg (FDR control). BH does not have a single per-test alpha, so the comparison must be empirical. Run a Monte Carlo (a few hundred experiments at each K) and report the BH ship rate on the primary metric vs Bonferroni's. BH should keep more power, especially at larger K.
  - Stretch: build the analogous power table for hierarchical Bayes with partial pooling. The model does not have a "per-test alpha" knob, so the comparison is via Monte Carlo on shipping rate against the same K. At which K does pooling start beating BH?
- Exercise 7: Two stakeholders read the same dashboard
  - Setup: a launch with these per-cohort lifts (from Exercise 2): most engaged 10 percent at -0.02, moderately engaged 30 percent at +0.03, long tail 60 percent at +0.06. Two stakeholders. Stakeholder A optimizes for the most engaged cohort (revenue concentrates there). Stakeholder B optimizes for the population aggregate.
  - Task: write each stakeholder's ship rule explicitly. A: ship iff most-engaged cohort lift exceeds 0 (one-sided z-test at alpha=0.05). B: ship iff population-weighted aggregate lift exceeds 0 (one-sided z-test on the pooled data). For each rule, run 200 simulated experiments at n_total=10000, seed range 1070 through 1269, and record the ship rate.
  - Expected pattern: stakeholder A ships rarely, because the most engaged cohort is small (n=1000) and the lift is small and negative on average. Stakeholder B ships frequently, because the population aggregate is positive. Same data, two decisions, both defensible under their own utility.
  - Two-lens: which stakeholder's ship rule is "correct"? The chapter's answer is neither: ship-rule choice is a values question. The lens choice (frequentist vs Bayesian) is downstream of the rule choice. Write one sentence on each.
  - Stretch: build a third stakeholder C who weights cohorts by an explicit value vector (e.g., most engaged worth 10, moderate worth 3, long tail worth 1) and ships iff value-weighted aggregate lift exceeds 0. Watch C land between A and B. The chapter's tradeoff_analysis story (Chapter 9, Loop D) generalizes here: pick weights, pick a posterior, pick a threshold.
- Carry-forward question: the chapter's big question was "which segments are worth segmenting on, beyond the surface demographic ones?". After these exercises, your sharper question is: when the cohort that hides the truth is defined by behaviour rather than demographics, how do I find the right slicing axis before I have run the experiment, given that retrospective slicing reintroduces the multiple-comparisons problem from Loop D?
