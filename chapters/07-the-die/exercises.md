# Chapter 07 exercises: The six-sided die

- Note: each exercise is small, runnable in the chapter's notebook or a fresh Python REPL, and probes one specific point the chapter raised. Two-lens exercises ask both questions explicitly.
- Exercise 1: Predict before counting, then watch the wobble
  - Setup: paper and pencil first. We will roll a fair die at N in {60, 600, 6000}.
  - Task: write down, before running anything, your best guess for the spread of face counts (max minus min) at each N. Then call expkit.sim.die.fair_die_rolls with seeds 70, 670, 6070 (chapter convention is SEEDS["fair"] + N) and report face_counts at each N.
  - Expected pattern: at N=60 the max minus min is typically in the range 6 to 12 (huge as a fraction of the expected 10 per face). At N=600 it shrinks to roughly 20 to 40 (small as a fraction of 100). At N=6000 it is a few percent of the expected 1000. This matches the count_wobble.png figure.
  - Two-lens: this is purely a data-generation exercise, like Chapter 1's running_fraction warm-up. The lenses arrive in Exercise 2.
  - Stretch: rerun seeds 0 through 4 at N=60 and check that the max minus min sometimes exceeds your prior guess. The chapter noted face counts are negatively correlated, so an unusually high face forces some other face down.
- Exercise 2: Chi-square goodness-of-fit, fair vs loaded
  - Setup: loaded probabilities loaded_p = np.array([2/15]*5 + [1/3]) (face 6 has p=1/3). Use the chapter's seeds: SEEDS["fair"]=70, SEEDS["loaded"]=71.
  - Task: at each N in {30, 60, 200, 600, 2000} simulate one fair-die roll set and one loaded-die roll set with expkit.sim.die.fair_die_rolls and loaded_die_rolls. Run expkit.inference.chi2.goodness_of_fit on both. Tabulate the p-values.
  - Expected pattern: fair p-values bounce around without trend, mostly above 0.05, occasionally dipping below (the 5 percent type-I rate). Loaded p-values start above 0.05 at N=30 and drop by orders of magnitude by N=600. The chapter records that the loaded p-value first sits below 0.05 from N=138 onward at seed 72. Your N=200 row should already be below 0.05 if you stay near these seeds.
  - Skeptical: search seeds 0 through 200 at N=60 (loaded_p, single experiment). Find a seed where the loaded chi-square p-value is above 0.20 (the test fails to detect the load at this small N). Print the seed and the p-value. Why is small N a chi-square's enemy here?
  - Stretch: replace fair_die_rolls with a slightly loaded die where face 6 has p=0.18 (only a small load). At what N does the chi-square test start rejecting reliably? Reuse expkit.power intuition: you are sketching power for the chi-square test.
- Exercise 3: Dirichlet posterior on the same loaded data
  - Setup: 600 rolls of the loaded die at SEEDS["loaded"] with the same loaded_p vector.
  - Task: compute counts with face_counts and the posterior mean with expkit.sim.die.dirichlet_posterior_mean. Also draw 5000 samples from the posterior Dirichlet(1 + counts) using numpy.random.default_rng(0).dirichlet(1 + counts, size=5000) and compute per-face 95 percent credible intervals from the samples.
  - Expected pattern: the posterior mean for faces 1 through 5 sits near 0.13 (slightly below 1/6) and for face 6 near 0.33. The fair line at 1/6 sits inside the credible intervals for faces 1 through 5 and well outside the face 6 credible interval. This reproduces the dirichlet_posterior.png panel.
  - Two-lens: chi-square asked "is the whole vector consistent with fair?" with one p-value. The Dirichlet posterior answers "what is plausible for each face?" with one credible interval per face. Same data, two question shapes.
  - Stretch: at N=60 only, the chi-square may not reject yet. Does the face 6 credible interval still exclude 1/6? At what smaller N does it stop excluding 1/6?
- Exercise 4: The multiple-comparisons trap, run the Monte Carlo yourself
  - Setup: simulate 1000 fair-die experiments at N=600 each, using a base seed of 72 (chapter's SEEDS["monte_carlo"]).
  - Task: for each experiment, run six per-face exact binomial tests against H0: p=1/6 with expkit.inference.binomial.binom_test_exact (use np.array of indicators is_face=face_k for each face). Record whether at least one test produces p<0.05 (naive) and whether at least one test produces p<0.05/6 (Bonferroni). Report the two empirical false-flag rates.
  - Expected pattern: naive false-flag rate around 22 to 23 percent, Bonferroni-corrected around 4 percent. This reproduces the multiple_comparisons.png numbers from Loop D.
  - Two-lens: the frequentist remedy here is Bonferroni. Compare against a Bayesian remedy: using your Dirichlet(1, ..., 1) posterior on a single fair-die experiment, compute the posterior probability that at least one face has p_face > 1/6 + 0.02. Is that probability close to 0.05? Explain the difference between "false flag" (frequentist test threshold) and "posterior probability of an extreme face" (Bayesian).
  - Stretch: replace Bonferroni with expkit.inference.multitest.holm and benjamini_hochberg on the six per-face p-values. Compare the false-flag rates against Bonferroni.
- Exercise 5: Holm vs BH on a mix of true and null faces
  - Setup: a 12-face die where the first 4 faces are loaded to p_each = 0.10 (slightly above 1/12 which is about 0.0833) and the remaining 8 faces share the leftover mass uniformly. Roll N=3000 times at seed 700.
  - Task: run 12 per-face exact binomial tests against p_null=1/12. Apply expkit.inference.multitest.bonferroni, holm, and benjamini_hochberg at alpha=0.05. Report the number of true positives (rejections among the 4 loaded faces) and the number of false positives (rejections among the 8 null faces) for each method.
  - Expected pattern: Bonferroni and Holm produce similar reject sets in this regime, with maybe 0 to 2 true positives and 0 false positives. Benjamini-Hochberg recovers more true positives (often 2 to 4) and may admit a small number of false positives. The trade is exactly what the chapter sketched: FWER control trims power, FDR control buys power at the cost of allowing a controlled fraction of false discoveries.
  - Two-lens: the Bayesian counterpart is hierarchical pooling, which the chapter says "we pick up in the hierarchical chapter". For now, note that adding more faces to the family does not change the Dirichlet posterior structure (one joint object), only the way you read it.
  - Stretch: redo with N=300. Watch all three methods drop true positives to near zero. The reason: at this N, no individual binomial test against p=1/12 has enough power to clear even an alpha=0.05 threshold, let alone an adjusted one.
- Exercise 6: Find a fair-die seed that would have lied
  - Setup: search seeds 0 through 999 at N=600 each.
  - Task: for each seed, run expkit.inference.chi2.goodness_of_fit on a fair-die simulation. Find every seed where the chi-square p-value is below 0.01 (a "very significant" rejection on a truly fair die). Count how many such seeds there are and print the most extreme one (smallest p-value).
  - Expected pattern: roughly 1 percent of 1000 seeds, so about 10 seeds, will produce a p-value below 0.01. The most extreme seed will likely have a p-value in the range 1e-4 to 1e-3. A truly fair die can fool a single-shot chi-square test about as often as the test's nominal rate suggests. Recording this seed gives you a cautionary tale: if a colleague brings you "the chi-square rejected at p=0.0006", ask how many times they ran it.
  - Skeptical: this is the multiple-comparisons problem in time, not space. Explain in one sentence how alpha-spending or pre-registration would defuse it.
  - Stretch: at the most extreme seed you found, also compute the Dirichlet(1, ..., 1) posterior. Does any face have credible interval that excludes 1/6? If so, which one, and how would a Bayesian narrate this case differently than a frequentist?
- Exercise 7: Two lenses on the same loaded die at one N
  - Setup: one loaded-die experiment, loaded_p as in Exercise 2, N=240 rolls, seed 71.
  - Task: from the same counts, compute the chi-square p-value (expkit.inference.chi2.goodness_of_fit) and the Dirichlet(1, ..., 1) posterior. Then do two specific tasks: (a) report the posterior probability that face 6 has p > 0.25 (sample-based, 5000 draws is fine), and (b) report the per-face Bonferroni-adjusted exact binomial p-values.
  - Expected pattern: chi-square p-value will likely be in the range 0.001 to 0.05 (reject the joint null). Bonferroni-adjusted face 6 p-value will be small (face 6 is the loaded one). The Bayesian posterior probability that face 6 exceeds 0.25 will be high, often above 0.85. The two lenses point the same direction here, but they license different statements: "the joint null is rejected" vs "face 6 probably has p above 0.25".
  - Two-lens: which lens would you put in the headline of a one-page write-up, and why?
  - Stretch: vary N from 60 to 6000 and tabulate, for each N, the chi-square p-value alongside the posterior probability that face 6 exceeds 0.25. The two curves should agree on direction at all N but disagree on rhetoric.
- Carry-forward question: the chapter's big question was "how do we go from 'is this one thing fair?' to 'is this thing different from that thing?'". After these exercises, your sharper question is: when I switch from one die against a fixed null to two dice against each other, do all three patterns (per-face wobble, multiple comparisons, hierarchical pooling) carry over unchanged, or does pairing introduce something new?
